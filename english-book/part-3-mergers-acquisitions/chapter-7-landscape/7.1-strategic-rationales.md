# 7.1 Why DAOs Merge and Acquire

The DAO M&A market is nascent — annual deal volume is measured in dozens of transactions rather than thousands — but it is growing rapidly as protocols mature, competitive pressures intensify, and treasuries accumulate sufficient capital to fund acquisitions. The investment banker who develops expertise in DAO M&A now will be positioned at the center of a transaction market that will expand by orders of magnitude over the coming decade.

## 7.1.1 Strategic Rationales for DAO M&A

### Technology Acquisition

The most common driver of DAO M&A is the acquisition of technology capabilities that would be expensive or time-consuming to build internally. Unlike traditional technology acquisitions where the acquirer obtains proprietary intellectual property, DAO technology acquisitions are complicated by the open-source nature of most protocols. If the code is publicly available, why acquire rather than fork?

The answer lies in what **cannot be forked**: the development team's institutional knowledge, the existing user base and liquidity, the protocol's reputation and brand equity, the network of integrations with other protocols, and the governance community that maintains and evolves the protocol.

{% hint style="info" %}
**The Non-Forkable Premium**

When MakerDAO created Spark Protocol (forked from Aave's codebase), it could replicate the code but had to independently build the user base, liquidity depth, and integration network. A direct acquisition of an existing lending protocol would have provided all of these immediately — at a price.

The acquisition premium over the cost of forking represents the value of non-forkable assets. This is the DAO-native equivalent of **goodwill** in traditional M&A.
{% endhint %}

### Liquidity and User Base Acquisition

In DeFi, liquidity begets liquidity. A DEX with deeper liquidity pools offers better execution, which attracts more traders, which generates more fees, which attracts more liquidity providers — a virtuous cycle. Acquiring a competitor's liquidity and user base can accelerate this flywheel more effectively than organic growth.

**Example:** Consider a scenario where Uniswap acquires a smaller DEX with $500 million in TVL concentrated in niche trading pairs. Rather than spending $50-100 million in liquidity incentives to bootstrap these pools organically — with uncertain results and the risk that incentivized liquidity leaves when rewards end — an acquisition provides permanent, organic liquidity with an established user base.

### Treasury Consolidation

Two DAOs with overlapping missions but separate treasuries can achieve more by combining resources. Treasury consolidation eliminates duplicated operational costs (both DAOs maintain separate development teams, security audits, legal counsel), provides a larger capital base for strategic investments, and creates negotiating leverage with counterparties (exchanges, market makers, institutional partners).

### Vertical Integration

Protocols that occupy different positions in the DeFi stack may merge to capture more of the value chain. A lending protocol acquiring an oracle network, a DEX acquiring a wallet provider, or a stablecoin issuer acquiring a lending market — each represents vertical integration that reduces dependency on third parties, improves the user experience, and captures a larger share of total value created.

{% hint style="success" %}
**Vertical Integration in Practice**

MakerDAO's creation of Spark Protocol is a vertical integration play: by operating its own lending market, MakerDAO captures the lending spread that would otherwise accrue to Aave or Compound, reduces its dependency on external lending protocols for DAI distribution, and creates a more seamless experience for users who borrow DAI.
{% endhint %}

### Defensive Acquisition

Acquiring a potential competitor before they grow into a serious threat. In fast-moving DeFi markets where new protocols can bootstrap significant TVL within weeks using incentive programs, defensive acquisitions — neutralizing competitive threats early — can preserve market position more cost-effectively than competing head-on.

### Governance Consolidation

Governance fragmentation — where multiple protocols with overlapping governance communities make redundant or conflicting decisions — can be resolved through merger. The Gnosis/xDAI merger consolidated two governance communities that were already heavily overlapping (many participants held both GNO and STAKE tokens) into a single, more efficient governance process.

### Talent Acquisition (Acqui-hire)

In a labor market where experienced smart contract developers, mechanism designers, and DeFi researchers are scarce and command extraordinary compensation, acquiring an entire team through protocol acquisition can be more effective than individual recruitment. The acqui-hire model, well-established in Silicon Valley, translates directly to the DAO context.

## 7.1.2 Taxonomy of DAO M&A Transaction Types

### 1. DAO-to-DAO Merger (Full Combination)

Two DAOs combine their treasuries, governance mechanisms, and operations into a single entity. This is the most complex transaction type, requiring governance approval from both communities, a negotiated token exchange ratio, smart contract migration or integration, and organizational restructuring.

**Precedents:** Fei Protocol/Rari Capital, Gnosis/xDAI

### 2. Protocol Acquisition by a DAO

A DAO uses treasury assets to acquire control of a smaller protocol. This may involve purchasing a majority of the target's governance tokens on the open market or through OTC deals, negotiating a governance proposal for the target to merge into the acquirer, or acquiring the target's treasury and development team through a structured transaction.

### 3. CeFi Acquisition of DeFi Protocol

A centralized entity (exchange, financial institution, technology company) acquires a DeFi protocol or protocol-adjacent company. Examples include Kraken's acquisition of Staked and Coinbase's hiring of protocol teams.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**The CeFi/DeFi Paradox**

These transactions create a unique dynamic not seen in traditional M&A: the acquirer obtains the development team but not necessarily control of the protocol they built. Token holders retain governance authority, and the protocol can be forked if the community disagrees with the acquirer's direction. Understanding this dynamic is essential for deal structuring.
{% endhint %}

### 4. SubDAO Creation and Spin-off

A DAO creates a subsidiary entity (SubDAO) to house a specific business line, function, or initiative. This is analogous to a corporate spin-off or carve-out.

**Example:** MakerDAO's Endgame plan, which creates multiple SubDAOs for different functions (lending, farming, ecosystem development), is the most ambitious example to date.

### 5. Token Swap / Strategic Alliance

Two DAOs exchange governance tokens to create mutual economic alignment without full merger. This is analogous to a strategic cross-shareholding arrangement. Token swaps create governance influence (each DAO has voting power in the other) and economic alignment (each DAO benefits from the other's success) without the complexity and risk of full integration.

### 6. Hostile Acquisition / Governance Takeover

An entity accumulates sufficient governance tokens to control a DAO's decision-making without the cooperation of the existing governance community. This is the DAO equivalent of a hostile takeover bid.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Low Governance Participation = Takeover Vulnerability**

Unlike traditional hostile takeovers, which require purchasing a majority of outstanding shares, DAO governance takeovers may require only a fraction of total supply if governance participation is low. A DAO where only 5% of tokens actively vote can be effectively controlled by acquiring 3% of total supply — a dramatically lower threshold than in traditional corporate governance.
{% endhint %}

## 7.1.3 The Scale and Evolution of the DAO M&A Market

### Historical Transaction Volume

**2020:** Minimal DAO M&A activity. Market focused on protocol launches and initial DeFi growth.

**2021 — The First M&A Wave:**

| Transaction | Type | Significance |
|------------|------|-------------|
| Fei Protocol / Rari Capital | DAO-to-DAO Merger | Combined algorithmic stablecoin with permissionless lending |
| Gnosis / xDAI | DAO-to-DAO Merger | Consolidated ecosystem with sidechain infrastructure |
| Rari Capital acquisitions | Protocol Acquisitions | Expanded Fuse lending platform |

Estimated total transaction volume: **$500 million - $1 billion** (notional, based on token valuations at time of announcement).

**2022 — Market Contraction:**
- Lido's expansion through strategic acquisitions of staking infrastructure on multiple chains
- Distressed protocol situations creating restructuring and acquisition opportunities
- The Beanstalk governance exploit highlighted governance takeover risks that inform M&A defense strategies

**2023-2024 — Market Recovery:**
- MakerDAO's Endgame restructuring (internal M&A — creating and funding SubDAOs)
- Growing institutional interest in acquiring DeFi protocols or protocol teams
- Increased cross-chain M&A as multi-chain deployment becomes standard
- Protocol teams being "acqui-hired" by larger entities
- OTC token purchases by funds seeking governance control positions

### Projected Market Growth

{% hint style="info" %}
**Market Projection**

As DAO treasuries grow and the ecosystem matures, projected DAO M&A transaction volume:
- **By 2027:** $5-10 billion annually
- **By 2030:** $20-50 billion annually

Drivers: consolidation of fragmented protocol categories, institutional entry creating well-capitalized acquirers, regulatory clarity enabling more complex transaction structures, and professionalization of DAO governance making deals easier to execute.
{% endhint %}

## Key Takeaways

- DAO M&A is driven by the same strategic rationales as traditional M&A, but execution is fundamentally different due to governance and open-source dynamics
- The "non-forkable premium" (team, users, liquidity, brand, integrations) is the DAO equivalent of goodwill
- Six transaction types exist with vastly different complexity and risk profiles
- Low governance participation creates hostile takeover vulnerability at dramatically lower thresholds than traditional M&A
- The market is projected to grow from hundreds of millions to tens of billions annually
