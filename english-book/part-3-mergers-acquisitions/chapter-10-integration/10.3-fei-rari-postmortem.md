# 10.3 Post-Mortem: The Fei/Rari Merger

The Fei Protocol/Rari Capital merger provides the most instructive post-mortem in DAO M&A history. The merger was announced in November 2021, executed through a token swap, and ultimately unwound by late 2022 following a catastrophic exploit. Every lesson from this failure should inform future DAO M&A transactions.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Learning Objectives**

After completing this section, you will be able to:
- Trace the Fei/Rari merger from announcement through failure
- Identify the five critical mistakes that led to the merger's destruction
- Apply these lessons to design pre-merger crisis protocols
- Develop risk management frameworks for future DAO M&A transactions
{% endhint %}

---

## What Happened

**November 2021:** Fei Protocol (algorithmic stablecoin) and Rari Capital (permissionless lending) announce a merger via token swap with governance integration.

**April 2022:** Attackers exploit a reentrancy vulnerability in Rari's Fuse lending pools, draining approximately **$80 million** from multiple pools.

**Post-Exploit:** Governance conflict between merged Fei and Rari communities over whether to reimburse affected depositors. The Rari community felt morally obligated; the Fei community resisted using FEI treasury funds for Rari-originated losses.

**Late 2022:** The merged entity unwound. Fei Protocol shut down and returned remaining treasury to FEI holders. The transaction that was supposed to create a stronger combined entity instead resulted in the destruction of both protocols.

---

## What Went Wrong

### 1. Insufficient Technical Due Diligence

The Fuse platform's permissionless pool design created an attack surface that was not adequately assessed during due diligence. The reentrancy vulnerability existed in code that was a fork of Compound V2, modified by Rari's team. The modifications introduced the vulnerability.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Lesson 1**

Smart contract DD must be comprehensive and must include independent assessment of **all code deployed under the target's governance**, not just the core protocol contracts. A line-by-line comparison of modified forks against original codebases is essential.
{% endhint %}

### 2. No Integration of Risk Management

The merged entity did not establish unified risk management across both protocols. Fei's stablecoin mechanism and Rari's lending pools had different risk profiles, and no combined risk framework was implemented.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Lesson 2**

Risk management integration should be a **Day 1 priority**, not an afterthought. The combined entity should have unified risk parameters, monitoring, and response procedures from the moment the merger closes.
{% endhint %}

### 3. Cultural Clash Over Liability

After the exploit, the two communities had fundamentally different views on liability. This unresolved cultural clash paralyzed governance and prevented effective crisis response.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Lesson 3**

Cultural compatibility assessment must go beyond surface-level analysis. Both communities should explicitly discuss and agree on **how they would handle adverse scenarios** before committing to a merger. Pre-merger agreement on crisis liability allocation is essential.
{% endhint %}

### 4. Inadequate Governance Crisis Protocol

No pre-agreed mechanism existed for resolving disputes between the two communities in crisis situations. The absence of a clear decision-making framework for emergencies left the merged entity unable to respond effectively.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Lesson 4**

Pre-merger agreement on **crisis response protocols** — who decides what, with what authority, in emergency situations — is essential. This should be a governance-approved framework, not a vague understanding.
{% endhint %}

### 5. Insufficient Contributor Retention

Key contributors from both sides departed as the governance conflict intensified, further reducing the merged entity's ability to address the crisis.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Lesson 5**

Contributor retention packages should include **clawback provisions** — if a contributor departs during a crisis period, vested tokens may be partially reclaimed by the treasury. Retention agreements must contemplate adverse scenarios.
{% endhint %}

## Designing a Pre-Merger Crisis Protocol

Based on the Fei/Rari lessons, every DAO merger should include a governance-approved crisis protocol:

### Crisis Triggers

| Trigger | Threshold | Authority |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Smart contract exploit | Loss exceeding $X or Y% of TVL | Emergency Committee activated |
| Regulatory action | Enforcement action against either protocol | Legal response team activated |
| Token price collapse | Decline exceeding 50% within 30 days | Treasury protection measures |
| Governance deadlock | Unable to pass proposals for 30+ days | Mediation process initiated |
| Key person departure | Loss of 2+ critical contributors within 30 days | Retention escalation protocol |

### Emergency Decision Authority

The Integration Steering Committee should have **pre-authorized emergency powers**:
- Pause affected smart contracts
- Deploy emergency treasury allocations up to a pre-approved limit
- Engage external security firms for incident response
- Issue emergency communications

### Post-Crisis Ratification

Emergency decisions must be ratified by full governance within 14 days. If ratification fails, the emergency measures are unwound (to the extent possible).

### Liability Allocation Framework

Pre-agree on how financial liability from crises is allocated:
- Losses from pre-merger code: primarily borne by the community that developed the code
- Losses from post-merger code: shared proportionally
- Treasury deployment for user reimbursement: subject to governance vote with pre-agreed parameters

### Contributor Crisis Obligations

Retention agreements should specify:
- Contributors cannot resign during an active crisis (defined period, e.g., 90 days)
- Departure during crisis triggers clawback of unvested tokens
- Additional crisis compensation for contributors who remain and contribute to resolution

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Fei/Rari merger is the most important cautionary tale in DAO M&A history
- Five critical mistakes: inadequate technical DD, no risk management integration, cultural clash over liability, no crisis protocol, insufficient retention
- Every merger should include a governance-approved crisis protocol with defined triggers, emergency authority, ratification requirements, and liability allocation
- Risk management integration is a Day 1 priority, not a Phase 2 task
- Cultural compatibility testing must include adverse scenario discussions — not just good-weather alignment
- Contributor retention agreements must contemplate crisis scenarios with clawback provisions
